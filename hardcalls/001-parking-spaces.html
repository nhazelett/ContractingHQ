Hard Call HC-001

The Parking Garage

Posted Jul 28, 2026 · Fictionalized from real events
DRAFT. This scenario is placeholder content. The facts and THE CALL will be rewritten before this goes live. The mechanics (poll, reveal, comments) are final.
1

The Situation

You are a warranted CO at a stateside installation. Headquarters sits across the street from a commercial parking garage, and parking on the installation side has been tight for years. In the last month of the fiscal year, a purchase request lands in your queue: a 12-month lease of 40 reserved spaces in that garage, funds certified, priced from a single phone quote provided by the garage operator. Well under the SAT.

The requirement document is one page. It says the spaces will support "recruiting, retention, and mission readiness" and will be assigned to senior staff and official visitors. There is no analysis of current parking capacity, no explanation of why 40 is the number, and no consideration of alternatives. The nominated COR is the commander's executive officer.

Before you finish reading the package, the deputy commander calls. The commander has already spoken with the garage owner, the owner is holding the spaces, and the front office would like award complete by the end of the month. The deputy is friendly about it. Everyone assumes this is a formality.

2

The Pressure

The money is certified and expiring. The dollar value is small enough that nobody upstream is going to look hard at it. The requiring activity owns the requirement, and you have been told your whole career that COs execute requirements, they do not invent them. Your rater and the commander talk regularly. A colleague who slow-rolled a front office request two years ago is still described as "not a team player."

And the commander has already shaken hands on it. If you stop this now, you are not flagging a risk. You are unwinding a deal your senior leader personally made, on a purchase everyone else in the building considers trivial.

3

The Authorities

Read these before you vote. They are the terrain the decision sits on. (DRAFT: verify cites before publishing.)

31 U.S.C. 1301(a), the Purpose Statute, and the necessary expense rule. Appropriated funds may be used only for their intended purposes. GAO's Principles of Federal Appropriations Law (the Red Book) has long treated commuting costs, including parking for employees, as personal expenses rather than necessary expenses of the agency, outside narrow exceptions. Read it →
FAR 1.602-1(b). No contract shall be entered into unless the contracting officer ensures that all requirements of law, executive orders, regulations, and all other applicable procedures have been met. The warrant is the last control in the chain. Read it →
FAR 3.101-1, standards of conduct. Government business shall be conducted in a manner above reproach, avoiding even the appearance of impropriety. A senior leader pre-arranging the deal with the vendor before the CO sees the package is exactly the fact pattern this language exists for. Read it →
FAR Part 10 and FAR 13.106-1, market research and soliciting one source. Even under the SAT, one phone quote from the vendor who is holding the spaces is not market research, and soliciting a single source requires a documented basis. Read it →

B. Stop and route it. Not because you are the requirements police, but because you are the last person in the chain whose signature certifies the action is lawful. FAR 1.602-1(b) does not have a carve-out for small dollars or friendly deputies.

Option A treats the warrant as a rubber stamp. The command owns the requirement, but the necessary expense question is not a requirements question. It is a fiscal law question, and it lands on whoever signs. If parking for senior staff is a personal expense, certified funds do not fix that. Certification means the money exists, not that it can buy this.

Option C feels principled but it skips a step. You do not actually know yet that no part of this is supportable. Official visitor spaces, fleet vehicle spaces, or a documented capacity problem might survive a fiscal law review. Refusing before the review turns a process question into a personality fight, and you will lose a personality fight with a commander.

So route it. Send the package to legal with the specific question, ask the requiring activity for the analysis behind the number 40, and tell the deputy exactly what you need to get to yes on whatever slice is defensible. Put dates on everything. If leadership wants to overrule the review, that decision gets made above your pay grade, in writing, and the file shows you did your job. That is what the warrant is for.
