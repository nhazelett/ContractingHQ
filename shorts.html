    The government cannot just pick its favorite company. A 1984 law, the Competition in Contracting Act, makes it compete its contracts.

    Competition is the rule, but there are seven legal exceptions. Each one, with an example and a trick to remember them.

    The simplified acquisition threshold

    At or under $350,000, fewer rules apply and small businesses go first. Go over it, and everything takes longer.

    Watch on YouTube (Episode 8, opens in a new tab)
  • 1:55
    Episode 09

    The micro-purchase threshold

    $15,000 for most purchases. At or under it, no competition is required, and it usually goes on a purchase card.

    Watch on YouTube (Episode 9, opens in a new tab)
  • 2:15
    Episode 10

    Small business set-asides

    When only small businesses can compete, who counts as small, and why the government will pay more on purpose.

    Watch on YouTube (Episode 10, opens in a new tab)
  • 2:49
    Episode 11

    Commercial vs. noncommercial

    Everything the government buys is one or the other. Commercial keeps the contract light. Noncommercial means more clauses and more rights for the government.

    Watch on YouTube (Episode 11, opens in a new tab)
  • 2:26
    Episode 12

    Davis-Bacon

    On federal construction, workers get at least the local prevailing wage and benefits. Contractors post it and pay it.

    Watch on YouTube (Episode 12, opens in a new tab)
  • 2:30
    Episode 13

    Service Contract Labor Standards

    Janitors, guards, cooks and groundskeepers on federal contracts get at least the local wage and benefits. A new contractor can even inherit the old one's union rates.

    Watch on YouTube (Episode 13, opens in a new tab)
  • 2:07
    Episode 14

    Firm-fixed-price contracts

    One price for the whole job. The contractor carries the cost risk and keeps the savings.

    Watch on YouTube (Episode 14, opens in a new tab)
  • 2:22
    Episode 15

    Cost-reimbursement contracts

    The government pays the contractor's allowable costs plus a fee, and carries most of the risk. It's for work nobody can price up front.

    Watch on YouTube (Episode 15, opens in a new tab)
  • 2:42
    Episode 16

    Certified cost or pricing data

    When there's no competition, the contractor shows the facts behind its price and certifies them. If the data was wrong, the government takes the difference back.

    Watch on YouTube (Episode 16, opens in a new tab)
  • 2:11
    Episode 17

    Fair and reasonable price

    Every price has to pass the test before the government pays it. Competition usually settles it. When it can't, the contracting officer has to show the work.

    Watch on YouTube (Episode 17, opens in a new tab)
  • 2:11
    Episode 18

    Types of contract modifications

    Two types, and the difference is who signs. Unilateral, only the contracting officer signs. Bilateral, both sides agree.

    Watch on YouTube (Episode 18, opens in a new tab)
  • 2:07
    Episode 19

    Fixed-price vs. cost-reimbursement

    Same job, two ways to pay. Fixed price puts the cost risk on the contractor; cost-reimbursement puts most of it on the government.

    Watch on YouTube (Episode 19, opens in a new tab)
  • 2:21
    Episode 20

    Constructive changes

    When the work changes without a written change order. The contractor did extra work, the government caused it, and the question is who pays.

    Watch on YouTube (Episode 20, opens in a new tab)
  • 2:12
    Episode 21

    Disputes and claims

    A claim is a written demand for a specific amount. Over $100,000 the contractor certifies it, the contracting officer decides, and the work keeps going during the appeal.

    Watch on YouTube (Episode 21, opens in a new tab)
  • 2:09
    Episode 22

    Inspection and acceptance

    The government inspects, then accepts, and acceptance is generally final. Defects it should have caught are on the government; hidden ones can come back.

    Watch on YouTube (Episode 22, opens in a new tab)
  • 1:51
    Episode 23

    Getting paid

    The Prompt Payment Act: send a proper invoice and payment is due in 30 days. Pay late, and the government owes interest automatically.

    Watch on YouTube (Episode 23, opens in a new tab)
  • 2:03
    Episode 24

    Options

    The government's right, and only the government's, to buy more or keep a contract going at a price set up front. Dash eight when you're late, dash nine when you're fine.

    Watch on YouTube (Episode 24, opens in a new tab)
  • 2:05
    Episode 25

    Termination for convenience vs. default

    Two ways the government ends a contract early, and who pays for each.

    Watch on YouTube (Episode 25, opens in a new tab)
  • 2:30
    Episode 26

    IDIQ contracts

    The prices and rules are set now, and the orders come later. A guaranteed minimum, a maximum, and a fair shot for every awardee on each order.

    Watch on YouTube (Episode 26, opens in a new tab)

    Episode 27

    The FAR

    The main rulebook agencies follow when they buy. It has the force of law, every rule has an address, and its clauses bind contractors.

    Watch on YouTube (Episode 27, opens in a new tab)

    Episode 28

    The Christian doctrine

    Some required clauses apply even when they're left out of the contract. A 1963 court case started it, and it's still used today.

    Watch on YouTube (Episode 28, opens in a new tab)
  • 2:11
    Episode 29

    Time-and-materials and labor-hour

    Paying by the hour when nobody can price the work up front, with a ceiling the government won't pay past.

    Watch on YouTube (Episode 29, opens in a new tab)

    Episode 30

    Blanket purchase agreements

    A charge account with a supplier. The terms are set once, the orders do the buying, and each order has a cap.

    Watch on YouTube (Episode 30, opens in a new tab)
  • 2:50
    Episode 31

    The GSA Schedule

    A store where the prices are already checked. GSA negotiates long-term contracts up front, so any agency can order fast.

    Watch on YouTube (Episode 31, opens in a new tab)

    Episode 32

    Fair opportunity

    When several companies hold contracts under one program, each gets a shot at every order above the micro-purchase threshold. Price always counts, and the exceptions are few.

    Watch on YouTube (Episode 32, opens in a new tab)

    Episode 33

    Federal employees and companies compete for commercial work, and the winner does the job. Congress froze it in 2009.

    Watch on YouTube (Episode 33, opens in a new tab)
  • 2:42
    Episode 34

    Sealed bidding

    Sealed prices, opened in public, judged on price alone. The lowest responsive bid from a responsible company wins.

    Watch on YouTube (Episode 34, opens in a new tab)

    Episode 35

    Some jobs only a federal employee can do. A contractor can research, analyze and recommend, but the decision and the signature stay with the government.

    Episode 36
