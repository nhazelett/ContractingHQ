Performance Assessment Without the Theater

Your part in CPARS: what the ratings actually mean, how to turn a year of surveillance into narratives that hold up when the contractor pushes back, and how to keep the assessment honest in both directions.

1 Your Part in the Assessment

Contractor performance evaluations are governed by FAR Subpart 42.11 and entered in the Contractor Performance Assessment Reporting System (CPARS), the official source for past performance information. Other contracting officers will read your contract's evaluations when they decide who gets their next award. That is the whole reason accuracy matters.

The contracting officer (CO) usually signs the evaluation as the Assessing Official. Agency procedures generally call for input from the people who saw the work, including quality assurance and end users (FAR 42.1103(a)). On a services contract, that is mostly you. Many COs assign the contracting officer's representative (COR) the Assessing Official Representative role in CPARS, which lets you enter proposed ratings and narratives for the CO to review. Your appointment letter or the CO will tell you whether you have that role.

Who owns what. You supply the facts and, if assigned, the draft. The CO decides the final ratings and releases the evaluation to the contractor. If the contractor disagrees, the evaluation is reviewed at a level above the CO (FAR 42.1103(d)(3)).

2 When Evaluations Happen

For contracts and orders above the simplified acquisition threshold (and construction at $900,000 or more, architect-engineer at $45,000 or more), evaluations are prepared at least annually and when the work is completed, and interim evaluations are allowed (FAR 42.1102). The CPARS Guidance sets a target of completing each evaluation within 120 days after the period ends.

None of that means you start writing in month eleven. The evaluation is a summary of a year of surveillance, and it is only as good as the records behind it. FAR 42.1103(b)(1)(iii) says evaluations have to be based on objective facts supported by performance data. Your surveillance records are that data.

3 The Factors, From the COR's Side

FAR 42.1103(b)(2) lists the factors each evaluation covers at a minimum. The table below lays out each one and where its evidence usually comes from in your file.

FactorWhat it asksYour evidence
Technical (quality of product or service)Did the work meet the PWS standards?Inspection results against acceptable quality levels, CDRs, validated complaints, passes as well as failures.
Schedule / timelinessWas work and every deliverable on time?Deliverable receipt dates, response times, missed or late services, and whether delays were the contractor's or the government's.
Cost controlDid the contractor manage cost? Not applicable to firm-fixed-price contracts.On cost-type or time-and-materials work: invoice review results, hours against estimates, overruns and their causes.
Management or business relationsHow did the contractor manage the work and work with the government?Responsiveness, how problems were reported and fixed, quality of corrective action plans, staffing stability, meeting records.
Small business subcontractingDid the contractor meet its subcontracting plan?Usually the CO's data, not yours. Applies only when there is a plan.

4 What the Ratings Mean

Each factor gets one of five ratings, defined in FAR 42.1103(b)(4) and Table 42-1. The table below gives the plain-language version of each.

RatingIn plain terms
ExceptionalMet the requirements and exceeded many of them to the government's benefit, with no significant weaknesses. Takes multiple significant, documented benefits, or one large enough on its own.
Very GoodMet the requirements and exceeded some of them to the government's benefit, with no significant weaknesses. Takes at least one significant, documented benefit.
SatisfactoryMet the contract's requirements. Problems were minor and the fixes appear satisfactory, or the contractor recovered from a major problem without impact to the contract.
MarginalDid not meet some requirements. A serious problem occurred and the contractor has not yet identified corrective actions. The narrative should point to how the contractor was notified, such as a CDR.
UnsatisfactoryDid not meet most requirements, and recovery is not likely in a timely way. Corrective actions were ineffective. One problem can be serious enough on its own.
Satisfactory is a good rating. It means the contractor did what the contract required. Table 42-1 says a contractor is not rated lower solely for not performing beyond the contract's requirements. Going above Satisfactory takes specific evidence that performance exceeded requirements and that it benefited the government.

5 The Theater, and How to Skip It

Most bad evaluations come from the same handful of habits. Each one makes the record less useful to the next CO who reads it.

  • Inflation. Very Good across the board because the contractor was pleasant, or because a lower rating means a fight. A pleasant contractor who met the contract earned a Satisfactory, which is a good rating.
  • Punishing one bad week. A single rough patch that was fixed quickly, in an otherwise solid year, does not make the year Marginal. Rate the period, and describe the problem and the recovery.
  • Rating the relationship. Business relations is about how the contractor managed the work and worked with the government, not whether you like the site lead.
  • Surprises. If the first time the contractor hears about a problem is in CPARS, something went wrong months earlier. Problems should have been raised as they happened, through the contractor's representative and the CO.
  • CPARS as leverage. Threatening a rating to get something done, or promising a good one in exchange for something, misuses the system. If performance needs to change, that is a CDR and a conversation with the CO.
  • Counting the government's problems against the contractor. If the contractor was late because the government did not grant base access or deliver the property it owed, say so, and do not rate the contractor down for it.

6 Writing Narratives That Hold Up

The narrative is what the next CO actually reads, and it is what a reviewing official weighs if the contractor disagrees. A rating without a supported narrative falls apart under a rebuttal. For each factor, write:

  1. What was required. The standard, with the PWS paragraph.
  2. What happened, with numbers and dates from your records.
  3. The impact, if there was one, on the mission, customers, safety, or cost.
  4. The contractor's response to any problem, and whether it worked.
  5. The bottom line, in the language of the rating definition.

Keep it factual and specific. Adjectives without evidence ("outstanding," "poor") add nothing. Leave out speculation about motives, events outside the contract, and anything you cannot support from the file. If the contractor submits a rebuttal, the CO will ask you whether it changes the facts. Answer that question with records, not with an argument.

Keep drafts inside the government. The contractor sees the evaluation when the CO releases it through CPARS. Do not share draft ratings or narratives with the contractor, and do not negotiate them. Send anything the contractor raises to the CO.

7 The Monthly Summary That Writes Your CPARS

The easiest way to write a good annual evaluation is to write twelve short monthly summaries. Many CORs already file a monthly COR status report in SPM; make one part of it a few lines per factor: what the surveillance showed, any problems and how they were resolved, and anything that exceeded requirements. When evaluation time comes, the narrative is mostly assembly. A template is on the Show Me tab.

The same summaries are what the CO needs for option decisions and interim evaluations, so the habit pays off more than once a year.

1 The Year in the File

This is the base year of the fictional custodial contract used across the COR track (firm-fixed-price, 15 restrooms and common areas in five buildings, plus Building 30 added by modification). The table below summarizes what the COR's records show.

AreaWhat the records show
Restroom cleaning (PWS 3.2.1)624 cleanings sampled over 52 weeks; 19 failed (3.0%) against a 10% acceptable quality level. No week exceeded the AQL. Every failure fixed same day.
Exterior trash (PWS 3.4.2)AQL exceeded in November (2 misses at Bldg 20, route change). CDR issued; contractor root cause and corrective action received in 4 days. No misses in the following 10 months.
Monthly reports (PWS 5.1)12 of 12 on time and complete.
Building 30 phase-in (P00002)Added with 2 weeks' notice; contractor staffed and began service on the effective date.
Beyond requirementsDuring a water main break in March, the contractor reassigned crews within an hour to support cleanup at Bldg 12 at no added cost; customer letter of appreciation on file.
Government-caused3 days of missed service in June when base access badges were delayed by the government. Not counted against the contractor.

2 Draft Narratives

Here is how that file turns into proposed ratings and narratives for the CO.

Technical (quality): Satisfactory.

PWS 3.2.1 requires restrooms cleaned daily by 0800 to a stated standard, with an acceptable quality level (AQL) of no more than 10% failed cleanings. Of 624 cleanings sampled during the period, 19 failed (3.0%), and no week exceeded the AQL; all failures were corrected the same day. PWS 3.4.2 (exterior trash) exceeded its AQL once, in November, with two missed pickups at Building 20 after a contractor route change. A Contract Discrepancy Report was issued; the contractor identified the cause and implemented a corrective action within 4 days, and no further misses occurred during the period. Overall, performance met contract requirements, with one problem that was promptly and effectively corrected.

Schedule: Satisfactory.

All 12 monthly reports required by PWS 5.1 were delivered on time and complete. Service at Building 30, added by Modification P00002 with two weeks' notice, began on the effective date. Three days of missed service in June resulted from government delays in issuing base access badges and are not attributed to the contractor.

Management: Very Good.

The contractor reported problems promptly and responded to the November CDR with a root cause and corrective action within 4 days, which was effective for the rest of the period. During a water main break in Building 12 in March, the contractor reassigned crews within one hour to support cleanup at no additional cost to the government, beyond PWS requirements; the customer documented the benefit to building operations in a letter of appreciation. Performance met requirements and exceeded some to the government's benefit.

Cost control: Not applicable (firm-fixed-price).

Why these ratings. Technical and schedule met requirements with minor, well-handled problems: Satisfactory. Management has a specific, documented example of exceeding requirements with a stated benefit: that supports Very Good. Nothing here supports Exceptional across the board, and the June delay was the government's, so it is noted and not counted.

3 Weak Narrative vs. Strong Narrative

The table below compares two narratives for the same technical rating.

WeakStrong
"Contractor did a great job keeping the buildings clean. There were a couple of issues with trash but they were taken care of. Very professional team." The Technical narrative in section 2: the standard, the sample size, the failure rate against the AQL, the one AQL breach with dates, the corrective action and its result, and a closing line in the language of the rating definition.

The weak version cannot support any particular rating, and it gives the contractor nothing specific to agree or disagree with. The strong version can be checked against the file line by line.

4 Monthly Performance Summary Template

Contract: [number]  |  Period: [month/year]  |  COR: [name]

1. Technical (quality): [inspections performed, results against each AQL, defects and fixes]

References: [SPM entries, CDR numbers, photos, correspondence]
