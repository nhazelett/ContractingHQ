COR Track • Staying Out of Trouble

The Stuff That Gets You In Trouble

Unauthorized commitments, scope creep, accepting what you shouldn't, supervising contractor employees, and ethics. The patterns COs see go wrong, why good CORs fall into them, and what to do instead.

1 How Good CORs Get in Trouble

Almost nobody gets in trouble as a contracting officer's representative (COR) by trying to cheat the system. They get in trouble by trying to help: keeping the mission moving, saying yes to a customer, giving the contractor a quick answer so the work does not stop. The intent is good. The authority is missing.

The boundary is in FAR 1.404(d): a COR has no authority to make commitments or changes that affect price, quality, quantity, delivery, or other terms and conditions of the contract, and must not direct the contractor to operate in conflict with the contract. Your designation letter also has to state that you may be personally liable for unauthorized acts (FAR 1.404(a)(2)(iv)). That line is not there to scare you. It is there so that nobody signs the letter without understanding where the edge is.

The one habit that prevents most of this. When a request would change what the contractor does, when, how much, or for what price, the answer is "Let me get that to the contracting officer." Said early and said every time, it keeps you, the contractor, and the customer out of nearly every problem on this page.

2 Unauthorized Commitments

FAR 1.405(a) defines an unauthorized commitment as an agreement that is not binding solely because the government representative who made it lacked the authority to make it. Two things have to be true at once:

  1. Unauthorized: the person acting did not have contracting authority for it at the time.
  2. Commitment: they actually committed the government, for example by telling a contractor or vendor to go ahead with work or a purchase on the expectation the government would pay.

Missing either element, it is not an unauthorized commitment. It might still be a problem worth fixing, just a different one.

SituationUnauthorized commitment?
COR tells the custodial contractor, "Go ahead and strip and wax Building 40 too, we'll sort out payment later." Building 40 is not in the contract.Yes. No authority, and a clear go-ahead with payment expected.
COR points out that PWS 3.2.1 requires dispensers stocked by 0800 and they are empty.No. Pointing to an existing requirement commits the government to nothing new.
A customer mentions to the crew that the break room could use extra cleaning. The crew does it on their own initiative, and nobody from the government asked for it or agreed to pay.Probably not. No one with or without authority committed the government. Still worth telling the CO, since it can turn into a claim or a habit.
COR emails a local vendor to deliver folding tables for a unit event, saying the unit will pay.Yes. The COR's appointment covers one contract, not purchasing.

When one happens, the government may be able to ratify it under FAR 1.405, an official with authority approving it after the fact, and agencies are supposed to take action to avoid the need for ratification in the first place (FAR 1.405(b)(1)). Ratification takes time and paperwork, and not every commitment can be ratified. The fastest, cleanest fix is telling the CO the day it happens. Most of these are honest mistakes made while trying to help, and they get handled that way. See Preparing Ratifications for the CO side.

3 Scope Creep and Constructive Changes

Scope creep rarely arrives as one big request. It is a series of small ones: "while you're out there," "can you also," "the last contractor always did this." Each one seems too minor to bother the CO about. Together they change the contract.

The risk is a constructive change. When the government's conduct causes the contractor to do work beyond the contract, even without a written modification, the contractor may be entitled to an equitable adjustment, meaning more money or more time. Common ways a COR causes one:

  • Asking for extra work that is not in the performance work statement (PWS).
  • Over-reading the PWS: insisting on a stricter standard than the contract says ("I want these floors mopped twice a day" when the PWS says once).
  • Interpreting ambiguous language in a way that adds work, instead of sending the question to the CO.
  • Accelerating: demanding work sooner than the contract requires.
  • Government delays or failures the contractor has to work around, such as late access or missing government-furnished property.
When the PWS is unclear, it goes to the CO. Resolving ambiguity is interpreting the contract, which is the CO's call. Your read of what the PWS means might be right, but if it adds work and the contractor reads it differently, a COR answer becomes the government's position in a later claim.

4 Technical Direction vs. Direction

Some contracts let the COR give technical direction: clarifying details within the existing scope. Your designation letter says whether you have that authority and how far it goes. Even where you do, technical direction cannot add work, change the price, or change the schedule. The table below sorts common situations.

Usually fineGoes to the CO
Pointing the contractor to the PWS paragraph and standard that applies.Any work not in the PWS, however small.
Providing information or access the contract says the government provides.Changing when work is due, or the order of deliverables in a way that changes the schedule.
Telling the contractor's representative about a defect against a standard.Telling the contractor how to fix it, which crews to use, or how to staff.
Clarifying a detail within scope when your designation letter gives you technical direction authority.Interpreting ambiguous contract language.
Passing customer feedback to the CO.Agreeing to a price, credit, deduction, or trade.

Put any technical direction in writing, copy the CO, and file it. If the contractor says your direction is a change, stop and send it to the CO.

5 Accepting What You Shouldn't

Acceptance acknowledges that the work conforms to the contract (FAR 46.501). It is easy to treat it as paperwork. The trouble patterns:

  • "Just this once." Accepting work that does not meet the standard to keep the peace, or in exchange for a promise to do better.
  • Accepting before checking. Clicking accept in WAWF on the invoice the day it arrives, before your surveillance for the period is done.
  • Accepting what your records contradict. Paying for services your own file shows were not performed.
  • Trading. Letting a missed service slide in exchange for extra work later. That is a change to the contract, made without authority.

None of this means acceptance should be withheld as leverage. If the work met the contract, accept it promptly. If it did not, tell the CO. See Invoice Review for CORs.

6 Supervising Contractor Employees

On a long contract, contractor employees can start to feel like part of the team. That is when CORs and customers drift into supervising them. Relatively continuous supervision and control of contractor employees is a key indicator of a personal services contract (FAR 37.201-2(b)), and agencies have to avoid making contractor personnel appear to be government employees (FAR 37.202-1).

Signs it is happening:

  • Assigning tasks to individual contractor workers instead of going through the contractor's supervisor.
  • Approving their leave, setting their hours, or deciding who works which shift.
  • Interviewing, selecting, rating, or disciplining them.
  • Putting them in government roles: government email signature with no contractor marking, government titles, a seat in government-only meetings.

Work through the contractor's designated supervisor or site lead. Tell them what the contract requires and what you observed; let them run their people. See Personal vs. Non-personal Services.

7 Ethics

You are a government employee with influence over a contractor's money and reputation. That makes the ethics rules apply to you in a very practical way. Your agency ethics counselor is the right person for any specific question; ask before, not after.

  • Gifts. Under 5 CFR 2635.204(a), you may accept unsolicited gifts worth $20 or less per source per occasion, up to $50 a year from any one source, and never cash. Coffee and donuts offered at a meeting, outside of a meal, are generally not treated as a gift at all. A holiday gift basket worth $75 is a different story. When in doubt, decline politely or pay market value.
  • Job talk. If the contractor brings up employment, or you are thinking of applying there, talk to your ethics counselor before any discussion. Seeking employment with a contractor you oversee can require you to step back from the contract.
  • Financial interests and relationships. Stock in the contractor, a family member working there, or a close personal relationship with its people can create a conflict. Your agency may also require you to file a financial disclosure report. Raise anything like this with your ethics counselor and the CO.
  • Information. Government estimates, other offerors' information, and source selection information are protected. Do not share nonpublic information with the contractor, and be careful what you say about upcoming recompetitions.
  • Favors. The contractor doing personal work for you, even "for free," is a problem. So is asking the crew to help with a unit event outside the contract.

8 When It Happens Anyway

Even careful CORs slip. What happens next mostly depends on how fast the CO hears about it.

  1. Stop. If work is underway that should not be, tell the contractor you are sending the matter to the CO, without promising anything either way.
  2. Tell the CO the same day, with the facts: what was said, by whom, when, and what the contractor has done since.
  3. Write it down. A memorandum for record while it is fresh. See The COR File, Demystified.
  4. Do not hide or fix it quietly. Deleting an email or striking a side deal with the contractor turns a fixable mistake into a much bigger one.
Early is fixable. A CO who hears about a problem the day it happens has options: a modification, a ratification, a conversation that clears up a misunderstanding. A CO who hears about it six months later at a claim or an inspection usually does not.

2 When It Slips: An Example Report to the CO

Suppose on Monday the COR did tell the crew to "fit in" the conference room, and realizes Tuesday morning that was a mistake. Here is the email that makes it fixable.

SUBJECT: [Contract number]: Possible out-of-scope direction, conference room, Bldg 1

[CO name],

Yesterday (24 Nov) at about 1400 I asked the contractor's site lead, J. Ramos, to have the crew clean the Bldg 1 conference room before Thursday's visit. The room is not on the PWS 3.1 cleaning list, and I did not have authority to add it. The crew has not started. I have told Ramos this morning to hold until he hears from you, and I made no promise about payment either way.

The commander still wants the room cleaned by Thursday. My MFR of the conversation is attached and filed in SPM.

[Name], COR

It owns the mistake, states the facts and times, says what the COR already did to stop it, and gives the CO what is needed to decide. That is what turns a slip into a non-event.

✅ Test Yourself

Five scenarios on staying out of trouble. Pick the best answer and check it.

Question 1 • Unauthorized Commitments
Is it an unauthorized commitment?

During an inspection, the COR tells the contractor's site lead that PWS 3.2.1 requires dispensers stocked by 0800 and three are empty. The site lead restocks them. Is this an unauthorized commitment?

Question 2 • Scope Creep
The commander's request.

The squadron commander asks the COR to have the custodial crew clean a room that is not in the PWS, "just this once, it'll take them twenty minutes." What should the COR do?

Question 3 • Interpretation
An ambiguous PWS.

The contractor asks whether "clean" in the PWS includes interior windows. The COR is fairly sure it should. What is the right response?

Question 4 • Contractor Employees
A leave request.

A contractor worker who has been on the contract for years asks the COR for Friday off. What should the COR do?

Question 5 • When It Slips
Realizing you made a mistake.

The day after telling the contractor to start out-of-scope work, the COR realizes it was beyond the COR's authority. The crew has not started yet. What is the best next step?
