COR Track • The COR File

The COR File, Demystified

What goes in the file, what stays out, how to keep it current without it eating your week, and what has to be true about it when you hand it off or the contract closes. Templates included.

1 What the COR File Is

The COR file is the record of everything you do under your appointment. It shows what authority you were given and what you did with it. When the contracting officer (CO), an inspector, a new COR, or a lawyer needs to know what happened on the contract, this is where they look.

In DoD, the Defense Federal Acquisition Regulation Supplement Procedures, Guidance, and Information (DFARS PGI) 201.602-2(d)(vi) says the contracting officer's representative (COR) maintains an electronic COR Surveillance file in the Surveillance and Performance Monitoring (SPM) module for each contract assigned. At a minimum it holds:

  • a copy of the CO's letter of designation and other documentation describing your duties, and
  • documentation of the actions you took under that delegation.

Other agencies set their own rules and systems, but the idea is the same: your appointment, and the evidence of what you did with it.

Two dates to know. The PGI has the CO review COR files within 6 months of award and then annually, documented in SPM (PGI 201.602-2(d)(vii)(A)). And before closeout, the COR makes sure the file is complete and available to the CO (PGI 201.602-2(d)(viii)). For what the CO checks during a review, see Annual COR File Reviews.

Positive observations belong in the file as much as problems do. A file of nothing but defects reads like a failing contract even when the contractor is doing well most of the time, and it gives the CO nothing to support a strong evaluation.

3 What Stays Out, and What Needs Care

Assume anything in the file may be read by the CO, an inspector, the contractor's lawyer in a dispute, or a requester under the Freedom of Information Act, after the release review that process includes. Write and file with that in mind.

  • Opinions and venting. "This contractor is lazy" helps no one and can hurt the government's position. Record what you observed and measured.
  • Other contracts. One file per contract. A shared folder of three contracts' records is hard to review and easy to mix up.
  • Unneeded personal information. If a timesheet or roster carries Social Security numbers, birthdates, or home addresses you do not need, do not upload that version. When you do need personal information, protect it under your agency's privacy rules.
  • Contractor proprietary information. Pricing breakdowns, staffing details, and technical approaches the contractor marked proprietary can belong in the file when they relate to your duties. Keep the markings, share only with people who need it, and ask the CO before sending it anywhere else.
  • Source selection information. If you served on the evaluation team, those materials belong with the CO's source selection record, not your COR file.
  • Unofficial direction. If you find a draft email in which you told the contractor to do something outside the PWS, do not quietly delete it. Tell the CO; a fixable problem gets harder when it is hidden.

4 Keeping It Current Without It Eating Your Week

The difference between a COR file that takes ten minutes a day and one that takes a weekend at year end is when things get written down.

  • Same day. Enter the inspection, the call, or the meeting the day it happens. Details fade fast, and a batch of entries dated the last day of the month looks reconstructed, because it was.
  • One naming pattern. For uploads, something like YYYY-MM-DD_type_subject (for example, 2026-11-14_inspection_restrooms-wk2) keeps the file sorting in date order and makes things findable.
  • Turn talk into records. Phone calls and hallway conversations with the contractor are where misunderstandings start. A short MFR the same day, and a one-line email to the contractor confirming what was discussed, closes that gap.
  • Copy the CO on what matters. Anything about scope, price, schedule, a dispute, or a request for direction goes to the CO, and the CO's answer goes in the file.
  • Sign as the COR. In correspondence with the contractor, identify yourself as the COR so nobody mistakes your role or authority.
The MFR test. Could someone who was not there read it and know who said what, when, about which PWS paragraph, and what happens next? If yes, it is a good MFR. Templates are on the Templates tab.

5 When You Leave, or the Contract Ends

CORs change jobs, deploy, retire, and move to new duty stations. Contracts end. Either way, the file has to be in shape for whoever picks it up.

  1. Tell the CO early. As soon as you know you are leaving, so the requiring activity can nominate a successor and the CO can designate them and terminate your appointment in JAM. A gap with no COR is a gap with no surveillance.
  2. Close your open items. Finish pending inspections, get outstanding CDR responses into the file, and record the status of anything unresolved.
  3. Write a short turnover note. Where things stand, what is open, recurring problem areas, the contractor's key people, and anything the next COR should watch for in the first month. File it in SPM.
  4. Walk the successor through the file if you overlap, even for an hour.
  5. At contract end, make sure the file is complete and available to the CO before closeout (PGI 201.602-2(d)(viii)): final inspections, final acceptance, any government property returned, and your last status report.

Your authority ends when the CO terminates your appointment, not when you clear the building. Until then, the contract is still yours to watch, and once it ends, do not act as the COR even if the contractor still calls you.

2 Memorandum for Record Template

Use this for phone calls, site conversations, and anything else that happened without paper.

MEMORANDUM FOR RECORD

SUBJECT: [Contract number], [short description of the event]

1. Date, time, and place: [when and where it happened]
2. Participants: [names, organizations, and roles, for example "J. Ramos, contractor site lead"]
3. Purpose: [why the conversation happened]
4. Discussion: [facts only: what was raised, by whom, the PWS or contract paragraph involved]
5. Outcome and follow-up: [what was agreed within COR authority, what was referred to the CO, who does what by when]

[Name], COR
[Date]

3 Weak MFR vs. Strong MFR

The table below compares two records of the same phone call.

WeakStrong
"Talked to contractor about the trash issue. They said they'd fix it. Will monitor." "21 Nov 2026, 1015, phone. Participants: [COR name], COR; J. Ramos, contractor site lead. Purpose: discuss two missed exterior trash pickups at Bldg 20 (PWS 3.4.2) on 12 and 19 Nov. Discussion: Ramos stated the route driver was reassigned and Bldg 20 was dropped from the new route sheet. COR restated the PWS 3.4.2 requirement (Mon/Wed/Fri by 1600) and did not discuss remedies. Outcome: Ramos stated Bldg 20 was added back to the route effective 22 Nov. COR will inspect Bldg 20 on each pickup day through 6 Dec. CO notified by email 21 Nov, 1040; CDR 01 issued by CO separately."

The strong version would hold up in a dispute a year from now. It also shows the COR stayed inside COR authority: restated the standard, left remedies to the CO, and told the CO the same morning.

4 Meeting Minutes Template

MEETING MINUTES

Contract: [number]  |  Meeting: [type, for example monthly performance review]  |  Date/time/location: [ ]

Attendees: [name, organization, role for each]

1. Performance since last meeting: [summary tied to QASP results]
2. Open items and CDRs: [status of each]
3. Contractor concerns raised: [facts; note any referred to the CO]
4. Government concerns raised: [facts]
5. Action items: [item, owner, due date]

Note: No changes to contract scope, price, or schedule were made at this meeting. Any such matters were referred to the contracting officer.

Prepared by: [Name], COR  |  Distributed to: [CO, attendees]

The closing note is worth keeping on every set of minutes. It records, in the moment, that the meeting did not change the contract.

✅ Test Yourself

Five scenarios on the COR file. Pick the best answer and check it.

Question 1 • What Goes In
Recording only problems.

A COR records every defect in detail but does not record inspections where everything met the standard, figuring passes are not worth the time. What is the problem with that file?

Question 2 • Conversations
A phone call with the contractor.

The contractor's site lead calls the COR to explain why a deliverable will be two days late and asks if that is okay. What should the COR do?

Question 3 • Personal Information
A timesheet with SSNs.

On a labor-hour contract, the contractor's timesheets list each worker's full Social Security number. The COR needs the hours to check invoices. What should go in the COR file?

Question 4 • Leaving the Job
Orders in hand.

A COR gets orders to a new base in six weeks. What is the best first step for the COR file?

Question 5 • Contract Closeout
The contract is ending.

The final option year ends next month. Under DFARS PGI 201.602-2(d)(viii), what does the COR need to make sure of before closeout?
