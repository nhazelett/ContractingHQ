About Second Look

What it is

A standard for reviewing a contract file, and a tool for applying it. Twelve questions cover the life of a contract: three before award, four at award, five after. Each question asks whether the file supports a decision the contracting officer made. The reviewer reads the file cold, records what is there, and grades only what the file does not support.

Why it was built

The FAR tells a contracting officer what has to be done. It does not tell a reviewer what to look at. Without a standard, a review tends to become a second compliance check: a search for documents and clauses the contracting officer and the contracting office are already responsible for. That adds time to the acquisition and little judgment to the file.

The record on where awards fail points somewhere else. In its reports to Congress for fiscal years 2024 and 2025, the Government Accountability Office listed the most common reasons it sustained protests: unreasonable technical evaluation, unreasonable cost or price evaluation, a flawed selection decision, and unreasonable rejection of a proposal. Each one is a failure of reasoning the file could not support. None is a missing form.

How it was designed

  • Structure: the Contract Management Standard. The National Contract Management Association publishes it as an American National Standard (ANSI/NCMA ASD 1-2019, reaffirmed 2025), and DoD built its contracting competency model on it. It divides contract management into pre-award, award, and post-award, with competencies and job tasks under each. Second Look uses the same three phases, and every question names the competency it sits under. The standard names the work. It does not say how to judge whether the work was done well, which is the gap this tool fills.
  • Focus: what DoD peer reviews examine. DFARS PGI 201.170-3 lists the elements a peer review team looks at: whether the process was understood by Government and industry, whether the source selection followed the plan and the solicitation, whether the evaluation and the decision were clearly documented and consistent with the stated criteria, and the business arrangement. None of them is a clause check, and peer review results are advisory. The questions here follow that lead.
  • Where awards fail: the protest record. The Government Accountability Office reports above set the weight. The award questions on evaluation and price are where a reviewer's time is most likely to matter.
  • Grading: existing practice. Critical, Substantive, and Administrative follow common DoD comment-matrix practice and line up with the Air Force CASPER matrix, so an Air Force reviewer can run a CASPER review with this tool.
  • Cites: current text. Every regulatory cite points to the Revolutionary FAR Overhaul model deviation text and the DoD class deviations, checked against acquisition.gov.

Three things are this tool's own and are not drawn from those sources: the cold-reader test, the three rules for findings, and the wording of the twelve questions.

The test

Could someone seeing this file for the first time follow the decision and defend it from the file alone? That reader might be a protest attorney, an auditor, or a member of the public. A finding names a decision the file does not support. It does not name a missing document, and it does not second-guess a choice that was the contracting officer's to make.

What it does

  • Reviews judgment: whether the reasoning in the file holds.
  • Asks the same twelve questions at every level, from a peer in the office to a headquarters review to a contracting officer checking their own file.
  • Keeps a record. Each answer carries a note on what the reviewer saw, so the review itself can be read and checked.
  • Ignores format. It asks what the file shows and never asks for a document by name.

What it is not

  • A compliance checklist. Whether required documents and clauses are present is the contracting officer's and the contracting office's work. The one exception is the award document, in the fourth award question, because the award is what binds the Government.
  • A way to overrule the contracting officer. If the file supports the choice, the answer is Supported. A reviewer's preference goes in a recommendation the contracting officer can dismiss.
  • An approval, a clearance, or a legal review.
  • Policy. No regulation or agency requires it. It is offered as a standard that a review at any level can follow, and it is written to work for any agency.

Your review

How this review works

The test

Read the file cold, the way a protest attorney or a member of the public would. For each question, ask one thing: could someone seeing this for the first time follow the decision and defend it from the file alone?

Three rules

  1. A finding names a decision the file does not support. It does not name a missing document. "Nothing in the file says why labor-hour was chosen" is a finding. "No determination and findings" is an administrative note at most.
  2. The contracting officer's discretion is not under review. If the file supports the choice, the answer is Supported, even when you would have chosen differently.
  3. How you would have done it goes in the recommendation box. The contracting officer can dismiss a recommendation without a response.

What is left to the contracting officer and the office

Compliance. This review assumes the contracting officer and the contracting office have checked that required documents exist and required clauses are in. The one exception is the award document, in the fourth award question, because the award is what binds the Government and everything else in the file leads up to it or follows from it.

For each question

  • Where to aim lists the places the answer usually turns on for this kind of buy. It is a guide for reading, not a list to answer line by line.
  • What I saw is your record: what the contracting officer decided and what in the file supports it. Write it for every question you answer. It is what makes the review worth reading.
  • Answer. Not supported, Supported, or Strong. Rest on an answer or tab to it to see what it means.
  • Leave offeror names and prices out. Use Offeror A, B, C.

Grading what the file does not support

  • Critical. Illegal, certain to lose a protest, or a risk the mission cannot carry. The acquisition stops until it is fixed, and the reviewer concurs on the fix.
  • Substantive. Could lose a protest or put execution at risk. The contracting officer corrects or addresses it and proceeds. No second review is needed.
  • Administrative. Small gaps that would not change the outcome if protested, plus grammar and format. Can be dismissed, and the acquisition proceeds.
  • Recommendation. How the reviewer would do it. The contracting officer's discretion, with no response required.

Where the line falls on a missing document

  • The document is missing and the file supports the decision somewhere else: Administrative.
  • The decision is not supported anywhere in the file: Substantive or Critical, by what rides on it.
  • The document is what grants the authority, such as an approved sole-source justification: Critical. Nothing else in the file can stand in for it.

Set by your agency, not the FAR

  • Which buys get an independent review, at what dollar value, and who the reviewer is. Air Force example: CASPER applies to operational buys at $5 million and up. The reviewer (the CASPER Risk Advisor) is the chief of the contracting office or a designee from $5 million to $10 million, the senior contracting official or a designee over $10 million to under $1 billion, and the head of the contracting activity or a designee at $1 billion and up. A designee sits at least one level above the contracting officer. Acquisitions already underway can stay on legacy business and contract clearance.
  • When the reviews happen. Air Force example: an Acquisition Strategy Review before the acquisition plan is final, a Pricing and Negotiation Review before negotiations or the competitive range, and an optional Contract Execution Review after award. After the strategy review, the reviewer sets the scope of later phases or waives them.
  • What is exempt. Air Force example: funding-only and administrative modifications, award or incentive fee modifications paid per the plan, labor standards wage adjustments, competitive orders under multiple-award contracts and schedules with no specific evaluation criteria and no new or changed terms, and mandatory sources.
  • Legal review. Air Force example: solicitations and proposed contracts over $3 million for operational contracting, justifications over $10 million, and source selection decisions and terminations at any value. The senior contracting official can change the dollar thresholds with local legal counsel. This tool does not replace legal review.
  • Rating scales and source selection document formats. DoD example: the DoD Source Selection Procedures referenced at DFARS PGI 215.200.

Outside this tool

Sealed bidding, architect-engineer selection, broad agency announcements, other transactions, undefinitized contract actions, major system acquisitions, and DoD peer reviews. Those reviews carry questions this set does not cover.

Note. Cites point to the Revolutionary FAR Overhaul (RFO) model deviation text on acquisition.gov. DoD rows cite the DoD class deviation supplement text (RDFARS) where it exists. Federal Supply Schedule ordering procedures sit in GSAR subpart 538.71. Agency supplements and local policy can add requirements. Second Look is a reference for the reviewer. It is not an approval, a clearance, or legal advice. To check which documents a file needs, use the Contract File Checklist.
