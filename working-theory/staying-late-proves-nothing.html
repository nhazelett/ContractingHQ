Staying Late Proves Nothing

Poor time management, trying to impress the boss, or something at home. Usually one of the three.

3 min read

KTHQ · Contracting

Profile and leadership philosophy to come.

I’ve noticed that people who stay late, night after night, usually have one of three things going on. Poor time management. Trying to impress the boss. Or something going on at home.

None of those is “they have more work than everyone else.” And none of them gets fixed by staying later.

1. Poor time management

This one is the most fixable, and it’s usually not laziness. More often it’s the perfectionist redoing work that was already done. Or the person who never learned to say no. Or the one who gets interrupted all day and can only focus after 1630, when the phones stop.

If you read the first piece, you know my version of this: redoing my whole team’s work because I didn’t trust it.

The extra hours also buy less than you think. John Pencavel, an economist at Stanford, studied munitions workers in World War I and found that output rose with hours up to about 50 a week, then flattened. Seventy-hour weeks produced barely more than 56-hour weeks.1 It’s factory data from a century ago, not office work, but the shape is familiar to anyone who’s rewritten the same paragraph at 1900. Past a point, you’re just doing tired work.

The fix isn’t more hours. It’s finding where the day is leaking. Protect a block of focus time. Stop redoing what was good enough. Say no to the thing that isn’t yours.

2. Trying to impress the boss

Here’s the uncomfortable part: this one often works. And that’s a leadership problem, not a worker problem.

Kimberly Elsbach and Dan Cable studied what they call passive face time, just being seen at work. People seen during normal hours got labeled “dependable.” People seen outside normal hours, early or late, got upgraded to “committed” and “dedicated.”2 And managers mostly didn’t realize they were doing it. They were rating presence, not work.

Erin Reid studied consultants at an elite firm where the ideal was an 80-hour week.3 Some of them quietly worked 50 or 60 hours and just made it look like they were always on. Managers couldn’t tell the difference. Both groups got the same rave reviews and promotions, and Reid found no evidence the people actually working 80 hours accomplished more.

So when staying late impresses the boss, it’s because the boss is rewarding the wrong signal. It’s the same trap as rewarding meeting attendance when you want technical experts. (More on that in Make Rank Because of the Work.) The fix is on the leader: rate the output, not the parking lot.

3. Something going on at home

This is the one to handle most carefully, because it’s the one where a leader can actually help.

Some people stay late because the office is easier than home. Money stress. A rough marriage. A house that’s too quiet. Sometimes the late nights are the first visible sign that something’s wrong.

The right move isn’t a lecture about work-life balance. It’s noticing. Then a quiet, private, “Hey, you’ve been here late a lot. Everything good?” Most of the time you’ll get a shrug, and that’s fine. They know you noticed. And if something is going on, you get them to the people who can help, the First Sergeant, the chaplain, the professionals, the same way I talked about in Dropping the Hammer.

The fair exception

Sometimes the work really does demand it. End of fiscal year in contracting. A deployment surge. A real emergency. Nobody’s arguing you should walk out at 1630 on September 30th with a stack of unawarded actions.

The test is simple: is it temporary, and is there a real reason? If late nights are the exception, that’s the job. If they’re the norm, it’s one of the three above.

Your people watch when you leave

If you stay until 1900 every night, you’ve set the standard, whether you meant to or not. Your people will assume that’s what it takes, and the ones trying to impress you will start staying until 1915.

So go home. Leave on time on purpose, and let them see you do it. Tell them you expect the same. Then judge them by what they got done, not by when they left.

The short version

  1. Staying late proves nothing. It’s usually time management, impressing the boss, or something at home.
  2. Past about 50 hours, you’re buying tired work. Find where the day leaks instead.
  3. Rate the output, not the parking lot. If late nights impress you, you’re rewarding the wrong thing.
  4. Notice the ones who never go home. Then ask, quietly, if everything’s good.
  5. Allow the real exceptions. Temporary, with a reason.
  6. Go home on time. Your people are watching.

Limitations: manual factory work a century ago, not office work. No direct quote used. Working paper (free). ↩

Evidence type: interviews plus an experiment; being seen at work during normal hours led observers to infer “dependable,” and being seen outside normal hours led them to infer “committed” and “dedicated,” usually without awareness. Limitations: corporate white-collar settings. Direct quotes are only the trait words. Journal article (DOI); plain-language version (MIT Sloan Management Review). ↩
