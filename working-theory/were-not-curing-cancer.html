We’re Not Curing Cancer

If you’re writing the contract that cures cancer, be stressed. The other 99 percent of the time, breathe.

4 min read

Nick Hazelett

KTHQ · Contracting

Profile and leadership philosophy to come.

I worked with a chief once who was always happy. Bubbly, outspoken, never seemed rattled. One day I asked him how he didn’t stress himself out.

He said, “We’re not curing cancer. If you’re writing the contract to cure cancer, be stressed out. Got it?” Then he pointed out that 99 percent of the things we do are not that. So relax.

I’ve carried that line for years. This piece is about why it matters, especially for the perfectionists, and why I think our whole career field needs to hear it.

Everybody thinks they’re the most important

Every career field tells itself it’s the most important one in the building.

Finance defends the budget; they’re responsible for billions. Security Forces defends the base; they’re the first line of defense. Contracting determines the fate of companies, in some cases literally.

All true. And the truth underneath it is that we’re all important. Nobody carries the base alone.

But let’s keep it on us in contracting. We’re nothing if we don’t have the people we support. Think about it. We have no mission without Security Forces, Force Support, CE, LRS. We don’t often write contracts to support ourselves. Everything we buy is for someone else’s mission.

That puts us in a unique spot. We touch almost every unit on the base, so we’re in a better position than nearly anyone to recognize how important everyone is. That should make us humble, not self-important.

There’s research on what happens when people actually see who their work is for. Adam Grant ran a study with university fundraising callers, a tough, repetitive job with lots of rejection. One group got to spend a few minutes with a student whose scholarship came from the money they raised. A month later, that group was spending 142 percent more time on the phone and bringing in 171 percent more money. The groups that never met the student didn’t change.1

Your scholarship student is the cop at the gate, the CE crew, the LRS troop. They’re why the contract exists. Remembering that does more for your work than another round of self-importance.

So let’s not take ourselves so seriously

Here’s the part some of you won’t like: at the end of the day, our job is simply to put work on paper and pay someone to do it.

Dumb it down.

Solicit. Evaluate. Award.

Or dumber: research stuff, ask for stuff, get stuff, pay for stuff.

That’s really the way it should be, and I think our culture needs to start changing in that direction.

I don’t need a 40-page requirements document to buy toner that has a part number. I do need a well-defined requirement. Clear isn’t the same as long. A janitor performing on a 100-page PWS is still a janitor on a 5-page document, doing janitor stuff.

If you’re a perfectionist, you can write the perfect contract in a year. Or you could throw together a simple, clear document, hire the right experts, and get the same outcome. The contractor is the expert in janitorial work. You don’t have to be. So what are we doing here?

Perfect doesn’t perform better

This is where the perfectionists need to hear the research, because perfectionism doesn’t do what we think it does.

A team of researchers pulled together 95 studies of perfectionism at work, covering about 25,000 people.2 They found no clear link between perfectionism and job performance. Perfectionists didn’t reliably do better work. What they did reliably have was more burnout, stress, anxiety, workaholism, and depression.

All the cost, none of the payoff.

If you read the first piece, you know I learned this one the hard way.

Stop being so damn hard on yourself

Do what you want to do. Have your own brand, your own flair. Build your documents the way you like them. That’s all good. Just remember the outcome we want: the customer gets what they need, the contractor gets paid, the mission keeps moving.

And stop being so damn hard on yourself for not remembering a D&F. Because the D&F did nothing. It didn’t make the toner show up faster or the janitor clean better. If it’s required, do it. Just don’t lose sleep over it. Fix it, learn it, move on.

If you’re worried that going easy on yourself will make you sloppy, the research says the opposite. Juliana Breines and Serena Chen ran four experiments on what happens after people fail.3 The people who treated themselves with self-compassion, instead of beating themselves up or pumping themselves up, were more motivated to improve. In one experiment, after failing a hard test, the self-compassion group studied longer for the retake than the others.

Being hard on yourself feels like accountability. Mostly it just feels bad. Being kind to yourself makes it safe to look at the mistake, and that’s what actually fixes it.

If you’re the leader

Your shop takes its stress level from you. If you treat every buy like it cures cancer, so will they. If you laugh about the toner order and fix the missing D&F without a lecture, they learn where to spend their worry.

Save your intensity for the contracts that deserve it: the ones where lives, the mission, or real money are on the line. Your people will believe you when it counts, because you didn’t spend it on toner.

The short version

  1. We’re all important. Contracting is nothing without the people we support.
  2. Remember who it’s for. Every contract has a customer. Go meet them.
  3. Dumb it down. Research stuff, ask for stuff, get stuff, pay for stuff.
  4. Clear beats long. A well-defined requirement doesn’t need 40 pages.
  5. Perfect doesn’t perform better. It just burns you out.
  6. Go easy on yourself. You’ll fix more, not less.
  7. Save the stress for the contract that cures cancer.

  1. Adam M. Grant, Elizabeth M. Campbell, Grace Chen, Keenan Cottone, David Lapedis, and Karen Lee, “Impact and the Art of Motivation Maintenance: The Effects of Contact with Beneficiaries on Persistence Behavior,” Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes 103, no. 1 (2007): 53–67. Evidence type: field experiment with university fundraising callers plus two lab experiments; a brief meeting with a scholarship recipient led to 142 percent more phone time and 171 percent more money raised a month later. Limitations: one call center in the field study; applying it to contracting customers is my comparison. No direct quote used. Journal article (DOI); Grant tells the story in Give and Take, free in Libby (audiobook). ↩
  2. Dana Harari, Brian W. Swider, Laurens Bujold Steed, and Amy P. Breidenthal, “Is Perfect Good? A Meta-Analysis of Perfectionism in the Workplace,” Journal of Applied Psychology 103, no. 10 (2018): 1121–1144. Evidence type: meta-analysis of 95 studies (about 25,000 people); no clear relationship between perfectionism and job performance, but consistent links to burnout, stress, anxiety, workaholism, and depression. Limitations: correlational; perfectionism had some upsides, like motivation and engagement. No direct quote used. Journal article (DOI). ↩
  3. Juliana G. Breines and Serena Chen, “Self-Compassion Increases Self-Improvement Motivation,” Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin 38, no. 9 (2012): 1133–1143. Evidence type: four experiments; people prompted toward self-compassion after a failure or weakness were more motivated to improve, including studying longer after failing a hard test. Limitations: student participants in lab settings. No direct quote used. Journal article (DOI). ↩
