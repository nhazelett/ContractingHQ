What COs Actually Evaluate

Every solicitation tells you how it will be graded. This page shows you where that is written, how to read it, and what a proposal looks like when it is built to match.

Home / Industry / What COs Actually Evaluate

Last updated November 2026. Written from the contracting officer's side. Citations follow the revised FAR (the FAR Overhaul model text).

The Short Version

The evaluation criteria are printed in the solicitation. Find them, read them first, and build your proposal around them.

The contracting officer (CO) does not get to grade you on anything the solicitation did not say would be graded. That rule works in your favor. It means the whole test is in front of you before you write a word.

Two places hold it. The instructions tell you what to submit and how. The evaluation criteria tell you how the government will judge it and pick a winner. In a traditional request for proposals those are Section L and Section M. In a commercial solicitation, which is most of what small businesses see, they are the addenda to FAR 52.212-1 and FAR 52.212-2, usually just called "the dash one" and "the dash two."

1. Where the instructions and criteria live

The format of the solicitation tells you where to look. The table below lays out the two formats you will run into and where each one keeps its instructions and evaluation criteria.

Solicitation typeWhat to submit (instructions)How you are judged (evaluation)
Commercial (SF 1449 cover page, FAR Part 12). Most supply and service buys a small business will see. FAR 52.212-1, Instructions to Offerors, plus the agency's addendum to it FAR 52.212-2, Evaluation, filled in by the CO (or an addendum titled something like "Basis for Award")
Uniform contract format request for proposals (Sections A through M, FAR Part 15). Larger or noncommercial work. Section L, Instructions, conditions, and notices to offerors (FAR 15.109-4(b)) Section M, Evaluation factors for award (FAR 15.109-4(c))

A few practical notes on finding them:

  • The base provision is boilerplate. The addendum is the real content. 52.212-1 on its own is generic text about late submissions, debriefings, and similar housekeeping. The part written for this specific buy is the addendum, which says exactly what goes in your package. Same with 52.212-2: the base text has blanks the CO fills in with the actual factors.
  • Check the attachments list. Many solicitations put the addenda in separate attachments ("Attachment 3, Instructions to Offerors" or similar) instead of in the body. The SAM.gov notice usually lists every attachment.
  • Combined synopsis/solicitations are short on purpose. For commercial buys the CO can post the whole solicitation as a single SAM.gov notice. The instructions and evaluation criteria are usually a few paragraphs in the notice text itself. Read the full description, not just the title and dates.
  • Amendments can change both. If the government amends the solicitation, reread the instructions and criteria in the amendment. Acknowledge every amendment the way the solicitation tells you to.
Quote or proposal? A request for quotations (RFQ) asks for quotes; a request for proposals (RFP) asks for offers. Under the commercial simplified procedures, FAR 12.203(c) says the evaluation of quotations is not subject to the formal Part 15 or Part 14 rules, so the process can be lighter and faster. You are still graded only on the basis for award the solicitation states (FAR 12.203(b)).

2. Read the evaluation criteria first

Most people start with the instructions because that is where the formatting rules and due date are. Start with the evaluation criteria instead (Section M or 52.212-2). It tells you what earns credit, which tells you what is worth your time to write.

When you read the criteria, pull out four things:

  1. The factors and subfactors. Technical, past performance, and price are the most common. FAR 12.203(a) says commercial evaluation factors need not be more detailed than those three, so many commercial buys stop there.
  2. The order of importance. For RFPs, FAR 15.104(d) requires the solicitation to say whether all the non-price factors combined are significantly more important than price, approximately equal to price, or significantly less important than price. That one sentence tells you whether to spend your effort on a better technical approach or a sharper price.
  3. The award method. Lowest price technically acceptable, tradeoff, or a simplified "best value" basis. The next section covers what each one means for you.
  4. The rating scheme, if one is stated. Acceptable/unacceptable, adjectival ratings (Outstanding, Good, Acceptable, and so on), or confidence ratings for past performance. The definitions tell you exactly what separates a "Good" from an "Acceptable."

Then read the instructions (Section L or the 52.212-1 addendum) and match every evaluation element to the place in your proposal where you will answer it. That match is your compliance matrix, covered in Example 2 (the services proposal).

From the CO's chair

The evaluators score what is on the page against what the criteria say. They are not allowed to fill in gaps from what they know about your company, your website, or a conversation at an industry day. If the criteria ask how you will staff weekend coverage and your proposal does not say, there is nothing for them to give you credit for.

3. How the award decision gets made

The award method changes how you should write. The table below lays out the methods you will see and what each one means for your proposal.

MethodHow the winner is pickedWhat it means for you
Lowest price technically acceptable (LPTA)
FAR 15.103-2		 Every offer is judged pass/fail against the minimum requirements. The lowest-priced offer that passes wins. No tradeoffs. Exceeding the requirement earns nothing. Show clearly that you meet every minimum, then compete on price.
Tradeoff
FAR 15.103-1		 The government can pay more for a better proposal when the benefits are worth the extra cost, or take a lower price when the difference in quality is not worth paying for. Strengths matter. Explain what you offer above the minimum and why it benefits the government, in terms the criteria actually rate.
Simplified commercial basis
FAR 12.203(b), 52.212-2		 Whatever the CO writes in 52.212-2: lowest price, lowest price that meets the specs, or a best-value comparison of a few factors. Read the stated basis literally. It is often one or two sentences, and those sentences are the whole test.

Will there be a second chance?

Under FAR 15.203, the government can award without negotiations if the RFP says it intends to. Most RFPs say exactly that. Assume your first submission is your only submission and make it complete.

If the government does negotiate, the CO first sets a competitive range of the most highly rated proposals (FAR 15.204-1). Proposals outside that range get a written notice and are done. Clarifications, which are limited exchanges to clear up minor issues, do not let you revise your proposal (FAR 15.202(a)(2)(iii)).

4. The three factors you will see most

Technical (sometimes called technical approach, technical capability, or quality)

For RFPs, FAR 15.104(b)(2) requires quality to be evaluated through one or more non-price factors, and technical is the usual one. Evaluators look for:

  • Understanding of the requirement. You describe the work in a way that shows you read the performance work statement (PWS) or specifications and know what makes this job hard.
  • A specific approach. Who does what, when, with what equipment, and how you will check your own work. Specific beats general every time.
  • Consistency. Your staffing plan, schedule, and price tell the same story. If your technical volume describes six people and your price covers four, the evaluators will notice.

Product buys work the same way on a smaller scale. If the criteria say the item must meet the salient characteristics in an attachment, the technical evaluation is whether your product literature shows each one.

Past performance

For RFPs, FAR 15.104(b)(3)(i) requires past performance to be evaluated unless the CO documents why it is not appropriate. It is an evaluation of how well you are likely to perform, based on how you performed on similar work. Evaluators look at:

  • Recency. The solicitation defines it, often work performed within the last three to five years.
  • Relevancy. Similar in size, scope, and complexity to this requirement. A $40,000 janitorial contract is weak evidence for a $3 million multi-building contract.
  • Quality. What your customers said, through questionnaires the solicitation asks you to send out and through government records such as the Contractor Performance Assessment Reporting System (CPARS).

The government is not limited to the references you list. Evaluators can look up other records too.

No past performance? FAR 15.202(c)(1) says an offeror without a record of relevant past performance may not be evaluated favorably or unfavorably on past performance. You get a neutral rating. That does not sink you, but it does not help either, so your technical approach and price carry more weight. Work you did as a subcontractor can count if the solicitation allows it; see past performance attribution in The Subcontracting Trap for the limits.

Past performance is a different question from responsibility

Past performance is an evaluation of how well you are likely to perform compared with the other offerors. Responsibility is a separate yes-or-no determination the CO makes about the apparent winner: can this company perform the contract at all? It covers things like adequate financial resources, the ability to meet the schedule, a satisfactory record of integrity, and the necessary equipment and facilities. When a CO finds a small business not responsible, the CO refers the matter to the Small Business Administration, which can overrule that finding by issuing a Certificate of Competency. Your SAM.gov record feeds this check, which is why reps and certs matter.

Price

For RFPs, FAR 15.104(b)(1)(i) requires price or cost to be evaluated in every competitive source selection. On a firm-fixed-price commercial buy, price evaluation usually means:

  • Reasonableness. Is the price fair and reasonable? Competition usually settles this. A price far above the others needs support.
  • Balance. Are individual line items priced sensibly, or is money shifted between line items or option years? Unbalanced pricing can make an offer unacceptable when it creates risk to the government.
  • Completeness. Every line item, every option period, in the format the price schedule asks for.

Is your price too low to be believable? On a fixed-price contract, the Government Accountability Office (GAO) has consistently held that an agency can assess that, often called price realism, only if the solicitation says it will. Check the evaluation criteria for language about evaluating whether prices reflect an understanding of the requirement.

5. Example 1: a commercial supply quote

This is a fictional combined synopsis/solicitation for portable generators, written the way many commercial notices read. First the addendum to 52.212-1 (what to submit), then the filled-in 52.212-2 (how it is judged), then what it tells you.

Addendum to FAR 52.212-1, Instructions to Offerors Quotes shall include the following. Quotes missing any item may be determined technically unacceptable.

(1) A completed price schedule (Attachment 2) for CLINs 0001 through 0003.
(2) Manufacturer product literature for the quoted generator demonstrating compliance with each salient characteristic listed in Attachment 1.
(3) A delivery date, not later than 45 days after award.
(4) The offeror's Unique Entity ID (UEI). The offeror must be registered in SAM.gov.

Quotes are due by 2:00 PM Eastern on the date in the notice, by email to the contract specialist. Files must be PDF and under 10 MB total.
FAR 52.212-2, Evaluation, as filled in The Government will award a purchase order to the responsible quoter whose quote is the lowest priced among those that are technically acceptable.

Technical acceptability: the product literature must demonstrate that the quoted item meets every salient characteristic in Attachment 1. A quote that does not demonstrate one or more salient characteristics will be rated unacceptable and will not be considered for award.

Price: total evaluated price is the sum of CLINs 0001 through 0003.

What this tells you

  • This is pass/fail plus price. A quieter, more fuel-efficient generator earns nothing extra. Meeting every characteristic and pricing sharply is the whole game.
  • The literature has to do the proving. "Our generator meets all specifications" is not evidence. Highlight or tab the spec sheet so each characteristic in Attachment 1 is easy to find. If the spec sheet does not show one, the evaluator cannot assume it.
  • All three contract line items (CLINs) count. Leaving one blank means the evaluated total is incomplete.
  • The housekeeping rules are real. A 14 MB email that bounces, or a quote at 2:05 PM, can take you out before anyone reads it.

6. Example 2: a services proposal and the compliance matrix

This is a fictional request for proposals for custodial services across several buildings, awarded on a tradeoff basis. The excerpts below are trimmed to the parts that drive the proposal.

Section L (excerpt), Instructions to Offerors L.1 Proposals shall be submitted in three volumes. Pages beyond the stated limits will not be evaluated.

Volume I, Technical (15 pages maximum). Address:
  a. Staffing plan: number of personnel by labor category, shift coverage, and how absences will be covered.
  b. Quality control: how the offeror will inspect its own work and correct deficiencies before the Government finds them.
  c. Phase-in: actions and timeline from award to full performance within 30 days.

Volume II, Past Performance. Up to three contracts performed within the last three years, similar in size and scope. For each, provide contract number, dollar value, period of performance, description of work, and a customer point of contact. Send the Past Performance Questionnaire (Attachment 5) to each reference.

Volume III, Price. Complete Attachment 4 for the base year and all option years.
Section M (excerpt), Evaluation Factors for Award M.1 Award will be made to the offeror whose proposal represents the best value to the Government, using a tradeoff process.

M.2 Factors: Factor 1, Technical; Factor 2, Past Performance; Factor 3, Price. Factor 1 is more important than Factor 2. The non-price factors, when combined, are significantly more important than price.

M.3 Technical will be rated Outstanding, Good, Acceptable, Marginal, or Unacceptable based on the subfactors in L.1 (staffing, quality control, phase-in). A rating of Unacceptable makes the proposal ineligible for award.

M.4 Past Performance will be assessed for recency, relevancy, and quality and assigned a confidence rating. Offerors without relevant past performance will receive a neutral rating.

M.5 Price will be evaluated for reasonableness and balance across the base and option years.

Turn it into a compliance matrix

A compliance matrix is a simple table that maps each requirement in the instructions and criteria to the exact spot in your proposal that answers it. Build it before you write, check it before you submit, and consider including it in your proposal so evaluators can find things fast (if it does not count against a page limit, or if Section L allows it). The table below is a compliance matrix built from the excerpts above.

RequirementSourceHow it is judgedWhere we answer it
Staffing plan: headcount by labor category, shifts, absence coverageL.1 Vol I (a)M.3 Technical, adjectival ratingVol I, Section 1, pp. 1-5
Quality control approachL.1 Vol I (b)M.3 TechnicalVol I, Section 2, pp. 6-10
Phase-in to full performance within 30 daysL.1 Vol I (c)M.3 TechnicalVol I, Section 3, pp. 11-14
Technical volume at or under 15 pagesL.1Pages beyond 15 not evaluatedVol I, 14 pages
Up to 3 contracts, last 3 years, similar size and scope, with all required dataL.1 Vol IIM.4 Recency, relevancy, qualityVol II, Refs 1-3
Questionnaires sent to each referenceL.1 Vol IIM.4 QualitySent; dates logged in Vol II cover page

What this tells you

  • Technical is where this one is won. Non-price factors combined are significantly more important than price, and technical outranks past performance. Put your best people on Volume I.
  • An Unacceptable in technical ends it. No price is low enough to save an unacceptable technical rating here.
  • Price still has to be consistent. The staffing plan in Volume I sets your labor cost. If Volume III cannot cover the people Volume I promises, the evaluators will see the gap, and it can hurt both factors.
  • Relevancy is defined as size and scope. Pick your three references for similarity, not just for the happiest customers.

7. What a weak answer and a strong answer look like

Using the staffing plan requirement from Example 2 (headcount by labor category, shift coverage, absence coverage), here are two answers an evaluator might read.

Weak. "Our company has over 15 years of experience providing high-quality custodial services. We will provide fully trained, qualified personnel in sufficient numbers to meet all requirements of the PWS. Our flexible staffing model ensures coverage at all times."

An evaluator has nothing to rate. No headcount, no labor categories, no shifts, and nothing on absences. "Sufficient numbers" and "flexible staffing model" restate the requirement without answering it. This kind of response usually draws a weakness or a deficiency, and the 15 years of experience belongs in past performance, not here.

Strong. "We will staff 9 full-time personnel: 1 project manager, 1 lead custodian, and 7 custodians. Day shift (0600-1430, Mon-Fri) covers Buildings 10, 12, and 14 with 4 custodians and the lead. Evening shift (1500-2330, Mon-Fri) covers Buildings 20 and 22 with 3 custodians. The project manager works day shift and is on call evenings. We maintain 2 trained part-time floaters who have completed site orientation; when a custodian is absent, the lead assigns a floater within 2 hours of shift start. Absences of more than 3 days are reported to the contracting officer's representative (COR) the same day. See Table 1-1 for the building-by-shift matrix."

Every element of the requirement is answered, the numbers can be checked against the price volume, and the absence process is specific enough to be rated as a strength under a tradeoff. It also reads quickly, which matters to an evaluator working through a stack of proposals.

8. What gets a proposal knocked out

These are the problems that most often move a proposal out of contention, many of them before anyone evaluates the substance.

  • Late submission. Under FAR 15.107(b)(1), a proposal received after the exact time specified is late and will not be considered except in narrow circumstances. Commercial solicitations carry the same rule through 52.212-1. Submit early, and confirm the email or portal actually received it.
  • Missing a required item. If the instructions say a missing item can make the proposal unacceptable, it can. A blank price line, a missing spec sheet, or an unsigned SF 1449 can do it.
  • Pages past the limit. When the instructions say excess pages will not be evaluated, the evaluators stop reading at the limit. Font size and margin rules count too.
  • Parroting the requirement. "We will comply with all requirements" or a pasted copy of the PWS shows nothing. The criteria ask how, so tell them how.
  • Taking exception to the terms. Conditions, assumptions, or changes to the solicitation's terms can make an offer unacceptable. If something in the solicitation is a problem, raise it as a question during the question period.
  • Inconsistency between volumes. Staffing in technical that does not match labor in price, or a delivery date in one place that differs from another.
  • Not acknowledging amendments. An amendment that changes price, quantity, quality, or delivery has to be acknowledged, or the offer may not be considered.
  • An ineligible SAM.gov record. An expired registration, or a size or socioeconomic status that does not fit a set-aside, can stop an award. See Registering in SAM and Set-Asides and Size Standards.
From the CO's chair

Ask questions. The solicitation gives a deadline for them, and answers usually go out to everyone as an amendment. If the instructions and criteria do not line up, or a requirement is unclear, a question before the deadline is far more useful than an assumption buried in your proposal. If you lose, ask for a debriefing; the instructions explain when you can request one, and the feedback is the best free proposal training there is.

9. Before you hit submit

  1. Every element in the evaluation criteria (Section M or 52.212-2) has a place in your proposal, and your compliance matrix shows where.
  2. Every item the instructions (Section L or the 52.212-1 addendum) ask for is included, in the requested format, volume, and file type.
  3. Page counts, font, and margins meet the limits.
  4. Your technical approach, staffing, and price match each other.
  5. Every price line and option period is filled in, and the totals add up.
  6. Every amendment is acknowledged.
  7. Past performance references are recent and relevant as the solicitation defines them, and questionnaires went out with enough time to come back.
  8. Your SAM.gov registration is active, and your reps and certs match what you are claiming.
  9. You are submitting well before the deadline, and you have confirmed receipt.
